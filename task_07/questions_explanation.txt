A:
Portugal’s GDP per capita grew slowly between 1900 and 1950 (from 1,997 to 3,869), reflecting an overwhelmingly agrarian economy (1900: “one of poorest Western European countries”, illiteracy above 70%), compounded by one state bankruptcies (1902), the 1910 collapse of the monarchy, and “rotating governments” through the 1920s. The 1926 military coup and Estado Novo (1933) imposed fiscal austerity and state-controlled industrial licensing (Condicionamento Industrial, 1931), which stabilized public finances but constrained growth.
Growth accelerated sharply from 1950 onward, peaking in the 1960-1970 decade (+72.2%). This period coincides with Portugal’s EFTA membership (1960) and the first moment industry surpassed agriculture in GDP contribution (1960)
Growth slowed sharply after 2000 (+24.8% in 2000, down to just +4.9% from 2010 and +6.6% in 2020), as economic stagnation set in from 2003. The 2008 global financial crisis, the 2010-2011 sovereign debt crisis (fiscal deficit ~11% of GDP), and the 2011 Troika bailout (€78 billion, severe austerity) explain the weakest decade of growth in the dataset (2000-2010, +4.9%).

B:
Throughout the 20th century, Portugal consistently trailed Spain, France, Germany, Belgium and Italy, typically sitting at 40-90% of their GDP per capita, with the gap narrowing steadily from 1900 to a peak around 1980-1990 (e.g., 91.2% of Spain’s level in 1980, up from 71.9% in 1900), reflecting industrialization, EFTA membership (1960) and EEC accession (1986). Greece was Portugal’s closest peer, with the ratio hovering around or above 100% for most of the century (117.7% in 1940, 115.6% in 2020), confirming the two countries’ similarly. Convergence with all five core economies reversed after 2000, with most ratios declining through 2010 before a modest recovery by 2020 - consistent with the 2008 financial crisis and 2011 Troika bailout interrupting Portugal’s catch-up process.

C:
Portugal converged with Western Europe almost continuously from 1900 through 1980-1990/2000, the strongest convergence period in the dataset: the Portugal/Spain ratio, for example, rose from 71.9% in 1900 to a peak of 91.2% in 1980. This acceleration followed the 1974 Carnation Revolution and culminated in EU accession in 1986, which opened markets and brought structural and cohesion funds. A second wave of convergence followed in the 1990s, driven by preparation for euro accounting adoption in 1999 and the physical introduction of euro notes and coins in 2002; GDP per capita reached 70% of the EU average by 2000, the overall convergence high-water mark recorded in the dataset. After 2000, however, convergence reversed: ratios against every comparison country declined through 2010 (Portugal/Spain fell to 79.0%, Portugal/Germany to 57.7%), coinciding with economic stagnation from 2003, the 2008 global financial crisis, and the 2011 Troika bailout. A modest recovery is visible by 2020, but Portugal has not returned to its 1980-1990/2000 convergence peak.
Unlike the other comparison countries, Portugal’s position relative to Greece remained broadly stable throughout the century (ratio oscillating between ~70% and ~115%, without a clear convergence or divergence trend), reflecting the two countries’ similarly peripheral position within Western Europe for most of the period.

D:
In 1990, shortly after the fall of communism, Portugal’s GDP per capita was dramatically higher than all seven post communist comparison countries: more than triple Romania’s (337.8%) and roughly double Poland’s, Hungary’s and the Baltic countries. However, these countries converged with Portugal remarkably fast over the following three decades. By 2020, Czechia had overtaken Portugal (87.0%, meaning Portugal was now below Czechia), and Hungary, Lithuania were near parity (97.8% and 90.5% respectively), while Poland (89.2%) closed the gap almost entirely. Only Romania remained further behind, though still converging sharply from 337.8% in 1990 to 106.1% in 2020 essentially reaching parity with Portugal. This rapid convergence reflects the post-communist transition reforms of the 1990s and, critically, EU accession (Poland, Czechia, Hungary, the Baltic states in 2004; Romania in 2007), which brought access to the single market, structural funds, and foreign investment. In relative terms, Portugal’s three-decade stagnation after 2000 contrasted with these countries rapid post-transition growth meant the gap that once separated Western and Eastern Europe narrowed dramatically, with several Central/Eastern European and Baltic economies now matching or exceeding Portugal’s income level.
